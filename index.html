1. Passwords

Why Password Strength Matters

Passwords are a common target for attackers because people tend to reuse them or choose ones that are easy to guess. If one weak password is exposed, attackers may use it to try to access more important accounts, including work systems, bank logins, or email.

  • Attackers use stolen password lists from data breaches.
  • They try the same password across multiple sites (credential stuffing).
  • Personal details (pet names, birthdays) make passwords easier to guess.

Examples of what attackers look for:

  • Password used more than once
  • Password based on public information
  • Password that follows predictable patterns (season + year, for example)

What Makes a Strong Password

The most important part of password strength is length and randomness. A passphrase — several unrelated words — is easier to remember and harder to break than a short, complex password.

Weak / Unsafe Password Examples

  • Summer2024! (predictable pattern)
  • Password123 (very common)
  • Ravens2019 (personal interest + date)
  • M!llieTheDog (pet name — easy to find on social media)

Strong / Recommended Passphrase Examples

  • ocean-window-leaf-drift
  • library-pine-travel-river
  • candle-harbor-orange-stone
  • Longer = significantly harder to crack.
  • Unrelated words make it resistant to guessing tools.
  • Easier to remember than symbol-heavy passwords.

Using a Password Manager

A password manager helps you avoid reusing passwords by storing them securely and generating strong ones automatically. You only need to remember one strong passphrase for the password manager itself.

  • Generates long, unique passwords for every website.
  • Stores passwords in encrypted form.
  • Autofills login information across devices.
  • Reduces stress of remembering dozens of logins.

Example workflow:

  • You create one strong passphrase: river-cloud-granite-sun
  • The password manager generates secure passwords like: F93!aYql0TZ)3$wLd4
  • You never need to type the complex ones — the manager fills them for you.

2. Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

MFA adds a second step during login to confirm your identity. Even if someone steals your password, they still cannot access your account without the second factor.

  • Something you know (your password)
  • Something you have (phone, authenticator code, or hardware key)

Common MFA Options

  • Authenticator app (e.g., Microsoft Authenticator, Google Authenticator)
  • Push approval notification (“Are you signing in?”)
  • Hardware security key (most secure option)
  • SMS text code (better than nothing, but less secure)

Example: Someone steals your email password. They try to log in — but your authenticator app prompts you. You deny the request → the attacker is blocked.

3. Backup & Recovery

If you lose or replace your phone, you need a backup way to log in. Planning ahead prevents lockouts.

  • Save recovery codes in your password manager or secure location.
  • Add a second authentication device if available.
  • Make sure recovery phone numbers and backup emails are up to date.

Example storage method: Store printed recovery codes in a sealed envelope at home in a private place — not in your wallet or desk.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Even with strong passwords and MFA, habits matter. Some behaviors can undo the protection.

  • Reusing the same password across accounts.
  • Using passwords based on personal or public information.
  • Approving MFA notifications you didn’t request.
  • Clicking login links from emails instead of navigating directly.
  • Storing passwords in notes apps, screenshots, or on paper near your desk.

Better alternatives:

  • Use a password manager for every login.
  • Decline unexpected MFA requests — report them.
  • Type website addresses manually or use bookmarks.

Key Takeaways

  • Use long passphrases instead of short, complex passwords.
  • Never reuse passwords — especially for email or work.
  • Use a password manager to make this easy.
  • Enable MFA wherever possible, starting with email.
  • Save backup codes securely in case you lose your device.

These steps significantly reduce the chances of someone accessing your account without permission.

1. Phishing

What is Phishing? Phishing (pronounced "fishing") is a form of identity theft that attempts to trick people into revealing personal or financial information online. Phishing scam attackers use phony Web sites or email messages that appear to be from trusted businesses and brands in order to steal personal information such as usernames, passwords, credit card numbers or Social Security numbers.

The perpetrator may also send an email message that contains malicious files, often Word, Excel or PDF documents. These malicious files can install worms or viruses on your computer. They can also install malicious software called “ransomware” intended to prevent you from accessing your computer’s files. Once the files are locked, the attacker demands a ransom, generally monetary, to unlock them.

Phishing & Email Scams: To protect your personal information and the security of Johns Hopkins and Center for Indigenous Health systems, please be extremely cautious about any email that asks you to follow a link and enter your login or other personal information.

Before you enter any information on a webpage that you believe is from Johns Hopkins or CIH, check the URL, which is the website address found at the top of the page. Johns Hopkins’ login page address starts with login.johnshopkins.edu. The URL of a fake page may include the words "Johns Hopkins" or "JHU" or "JHHS," but if it does not follow the format above, you are not on the actual login site.

Detecting a Phishing Scam: Some phishing email messages are obvious frauds, full of spelling errors or clearly phony attempts to suggest a previous connection between you and a message sender. Other phishing attempts, however, are quite clever and deceptive. The more familiar a message appears; the more susceptible people may be to performing actions suggested in the message.

One type of phishing attempt is an email message stating that you are receiving it due to fraudulent activity on your account and asking you to "click here" to verify your information. They might even send you to a phony but real-looking Johns Hopkins login page. So please be careful. Never judge a message simply by how real it looks or from whom it purports to come. Think also about what it is asking you to do. Look at the URLs of sites where the message is trying to send you.

Phishing scams often try to scare or trick the recipient into responding or clicking immediately by claiming they will lose something (e.g., email, bank account). Such a claim is always indicative of a phishing scam, as responsible companies and organizations will never take these types of actions using email.

Phishing Prevention: We will never send you an email message asking you to follow links to “verify” information about yourself. Likewise, responsible banks, credit card companies, retailers, social media companies and others who email you will never ask you by email to follow a link and input critical account or personal information.

Please remember, entering your user ID and password on a page you access from a link in a scam message gives phishers your credentials. They may then use this information to access your Johns Hopkins or personal information. That could result in identity theft, damage to your credit and other serious consequences. It could also result in attacks on other computers on the Johns Hopkins network.

Protect Yourself from Phishing and Other Email Scams: Dos and Don'ts:

  • Don’t send passwords or any sensitive information over email.
  • Don’t click on "verify your account" or "login" links in any email.
  • Don’t reply to, click on links in, or open attachments in spam or suspicious email.
  • Don’t call a phone number in an unsolicited email or give sensitive data to a caller.
  • Do report impersonated or suspect email to your designed CIH contact.
  • Do be cautious about opening attachments, even from trusted senders.

When You Receive an Email Message, Consider These Points:

  • Are you expecting an email of this nature? (e.g., password reset, account expiration, wire transfer, travel confirmation, etc.)
  • Do you do business with the company or person purportedly emailing you?
  • Does the message ask for any personal information (password, credit cards, SSN, etc.)?
  • Does the message ask for sensitive information about others?
  • Does the message ask you to immediately open an attachment?
  • Hover your mouse over the links in the email. Does the hover-text link match what's in the text? Do the actual links look like a site with which you would normally do business?
  • Does the "From" email address look like either someone you know, a business you work with, or a proper Johns Hopkins email account?
  • Click 'Reply' - Does the address in the 'To' field match the sender of the message?

2. Smishing

What is Smishing? Smishing is a form of phishing in which an attacker uses a compelling text message to trick targeted recipients into clicking a link and sending the attacker private information or downloading malicious programs to a smartphone.

Most of the smartphones in the world can receive text messages from any number in the world. Many recipients are already aware of the dangers of clicking a link in email messages. Fewer people are aware of the dangers of clicking links in text messages.

Recipients are much more trusting of text messages, so smishing is often lucrative to attackers phishing for credentials, banking information and private data.

How Does Smishing Work?

Most smishing attacks work like email phishing. The attacker sends a message enticing the recipient to click a link or asks for a reply that contains the targeted recipient’s private data.

The information an attacker wants can be anything, including:

  • Online account credentials.
  • Private information that could be used in identity theft.
  • Financial data that can be used to sell on darknet markets or for online fraud.

Smishers use a variety of ways to trick recipients into sending private information. They may use basic information about the targeted recipient, like name and address, available from public online tools to fool the targeted recipient into thinking the message is coming from a trusted source.

The smisher may use your name and location to address you directly. These details make the message more compelling. The message then displays a link pointing to an attacker-controlled computer. The link may lead to a credential phishing site or malware designed to compromise the smartphone itself. The malware can then be used to snoop the recipient’s smartphone data or send sensitive data silently to an attacker-controlled computer.

Social Engineering is used in combination with smishing. The attacker might call the recipient asking for private information before sending a text message. The private information can then be used in the smisher’s text message attack. Several telecom providers have tried to fight social engineering calls by displaying “Spam Risk” on a smartphone when a known scam number calls the recipient.

Malware is often stopped by basic Android and iOS security features. But even with robust security controls on mobile operating systems, no security controls can combat recipients who willingly send their data to an unknown number.

3. Gift Card Scams

Johns Hopkins faculty, staff and students are frequently targeted by cybercriminals using gift card scams. These scams lure our colleagues into buying gift cards using impersonation, often ‘spoofing’ someone of authority, to trick the victim into making the purchases. The cybercriminals initiate these scams through both email and/or text messaging. Many start with an email purporting to be from a colleague in authority asking something like, “Kindly provide your available cell number”, in an attempt to transition the conversation to text messaging. Many others are conducted entirely through text messaging. In most cases, both are characterized by a sense of urgency. If the victim responds, the conversation will quickly transition into a gift card purchase request that includes sharing card numbers and pins with the scammer.

Research Project and Field-Based Work Cybersecurity Practices

This section explains the expectations for safeguarding research data, participant identity, and organizational trust when conducting work in community-based settings. Because we work directly with tribal nations and communities, confidentiality and secure data handling are not merely technical requirements but relational commitments—protecting dignity, respect, and sovereignty. Cybersecurity in this context is about honoring the trust families, youth, Elders, and local partners place in us by sharing their time, stories, personal information, and lived experiences. Every team member, regardless of role or length of employment, shares this responsibility.

1. Protecting Participant Identity & Sensitive Data

Protecting participant identity is one of the most important responsibilities of anyone engaged in community-based research. In many communities, a participant does not exist as an isolated individual; they are part of a family, a household, and a broader network of relationships. Because of these close community ties, even very small details—such as a neighborhood, family structure, or a mention of a medical appointment—can indirectly reveal a person’s identity. Protecting identity therefore requires careful attention not just to what you store and document, but to what you say in public, where you use your device, how you manage printed materials, and how you speak about your day-to-day role.

Research staff are also responsible for following federal laws that protect privacy. HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) applies to health information that can be tied to a specific person (for example, diagnoses, screenings, clinical referrals, medication status, treatment engagement, etc.). If your research involves any connection to a healthcare provider, clinic, community health program, or any medical record, HIPAA standards apply. Protected Health Information (PHI) must never be stored on unapproved platforms, shared through personal messaging, or discussed casually. Likewise, research conducted in or with schools may involve FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act), which protects student education records and personally identifiable information about children and youth. If a research project engages with students, schools, teachers, parents, school-based behavioral health teams, or attendance records, student information is considered educationally protected and must be handled with the same strict confidentiality.

CIH’s research agreements with Tribal Nations also emphasize that participant information belongs to the Tribal community, not to any university, program, or researcher. This means that confidentiality is also part of honoring sovereignty. When community members share their information, they are choosing to trust the person in front of them—you or a member of your team. Protecting that trust helps maintain the integrity, reputation, and long-term collaboration of the CIH and supports the community’s right to determine how data are collected, interpreted, stored, and used.

Key Guidelines:

  • Treat all participant information—names, addresses, family relationships, medical history, screening results, school records, and household details—as protected and confidential under HIPAA, FERPA, and Tribal data sovereignty agreements.
  • Do not store participant information in personal phone apps, screenshots, texting threads, photos, notebooks, personal cloud storage (e.g., Google Drive, iCloud), or email drafts. These are not secure and put participants at risk.
  • Only access participant information when it is necessary for your assigned responsibilities—this is called the Minimum Necessary Standard, a core rule under HIPAA and best practice under FERPA.
  • Do not discuss participant details with anyone who is not directly part of the research team—this includes family members, friends, community members, and other professionals who are not listed on the study protocol.
  • If someone asks you about your work with a participant or tries to learn more, it is appropriate to respond warmly but firmly, such as: “I can’t share details about anyone I meet with, but I’m glad to see you. How are you doing?”
  • If at any point you are unsure whether something could identify a participant or violate HIPAA/FERPA, do not share it. Ask your supervisor or the project lead for clarification.
  • When in doubt, choose privacy, respect, and caution. It is always safer to protect more than required than to accidentally reveal information.

2. Device Handling in Community Settings

Field-based work means that staff often travel between homes, schools, community centers, and clinics—environments that are less controlled and less secure than office settings. This makes safeguarding your work laptop, tablet, or phone especially important. Devices can be lost, borrowed by others, accidentally viewed by people nearby, or left unlocked during a quick conversation or visit interruption. Your device is a gateway into the research data system and must be treated as a confidential work tool, not a personal computer. Keeping your device physically secure and digitally locked ensures that participant data and project materials are protected even if you are working in dynamic environments.

Key Guidelines:

  • Always use JHU-issued devices—never use personal laptops or phones for research work.
  • Ensure automatic screen lock and password protection is enabled.
  • Keep your device in your possession or in a locked location—never leave devices visible in cars.
  • Position yourself so others cannot easily view your screen.
  • If a device is lost, stolen, or accessed by someone else, contact your supervisor immediately.

3. Data Recording & Transfer Protocols

Many research projects use digital data collection platforms to ensure secure storage, encryption, and proper tracking of research records. Proper handling of data means using the approved systems every time and avoiding temporary workarounds that feel convenient in the moment but create risk. The biggest mistakes happen when data are saved locally on devices or when syncing procedures are not followed. Ensuring that your data uploads correctly before leaving a site—or as soon as you regain connectivity—protects against accidental data loss, duplication, or compromised confidentiality.

Key Guidelines:

  • Use only approved systems for entering data (like REDCap or Qualtrics).
  • Do not store data locally unless encryption and approval are confirmed.
  • Upload data after each participant interaction or as soon as reliable internet is available.
  • Verify that uploads were completed successfully before deleting temporary copies.
  • Contact your supervisor if you are unsure whether data uploaded correctly or encounter an error.

4. Secure Communications with Participants

Communication with participants often extends beyond scheduled research visits, but this communication must remain professional, respectful, and secure. Texting or calling participants is appropriate for appointment coordination, but it is not a place for discussing personal, medical, or emotional topics—especially given that many standard texting systems are not encrypted and can be seen by family members who share phones. Remember that participants may trust you deeply; maintaining boundaries in how you communicate protects that relationship.

Key Guidelines:

  • Use only project-approved phone numbers, devices, or messaging systems.
  • Texts and calls should focus on logistics only (e.g., confirming visit time or location).
  • Never discuss test results, screening outcomes, personal concerns, or medical issues by text.
  • Do not use personal social media, Messenger, WhatsApp, or other informal messaging platforms for project communication.
  • When in doubt, schedule an in-person conversation instead of continuing a sensitive text exchange.

5. Field Notes, Paper Logs, and Secure Storage

Even in digital research workflows, field staff often use notebooks, printed forms, or temporary logs to help track visits or participant details. Paper records create a special kind of vulnerability because they can be easily seen, misplaced, or accidentally mixed with personal papers. These materials must be labeled, stored, transported, and destroyed with careful attention so that confidential information is never exposed.

Key Guidelines:

  • Label documents using participant study ID only, not names.
  • Keep paper materials in locked folders or bags — never loose in vehicles or personal living spaces.
  • Return documents to secured office storage at your workplace as soon as possible after collection.
  • Do not store research materials at home.
  • Dispose of notes and printouts by shredding or returning to your supervisor or designated project lead for secure destruction.

6. Photos, Audio, & Media Files

Images and recordings can reveal identity even without names or obvious details — voices, homes, faces, and cultural items are all recognizable. Because of this, recording must be treated as a formal research procedure, not something casual or automatic. Photos and recordings should only occur with explicit consent and only for project-approved purposes.

Key Guidelines:

  • Do not take photos, screenshots, audio, or video unless participants have signed consent that specifically allows media recording.
  • Immediately upload recordings to an encrypted storage location like OneDrive per project protocol, then delete them from your device.
  • Never send media files through text, Messenger, AirDrop, or personal email.
  • Never share research-related media on social media, messaging apps, public presentations, or personal portfolios.
  • When unsure, do not record.

7. Cultural & Relational Context of Confidentiality

In many communities, confidentiality has social, cultural, and relational dimensions—people may check in out of genuine care when they see you working with someone they know. Responding respectfully while maintaining confidentiality honors community values and the relationships you are building. Confidentiality is not secrecy—it is respect for personal boundaries, relational trust, sovereignty, and dignity.

Key Guidelines:

  • Use warm, non-defensive language when declining to share information.
  • Assume inquiries come from care or curiosity, not intrusion.
  • Protecting confidentiality is protecting trust, not hiding information.
  • Confidentiality supports participants' sense of safety in seeking support or services.
  • Practice your response so it feels natural in community contexts.

8. Reporting Incidents & Getting Help

Even with the best intentions, mistakes can happen. Reporting concerns immediately allows the team to respond in ways that protect participants, honor relationships, and comply with research requirements. Delayed reporting can unintentionally increase harm — quick reporting allows quick correction. You will never be punished for reporting promptly.

Key Guidelines:

  • Report any lost or stolen devices, accidental disclosures, data entry mistakes, or recording errors.
  • Contact your supervisor or project lead with any questions.
  • Do not attempt to “fix” issues quietly or individually.
  • Reporting is an act of responsibility and care — not an admission of failure.
  • When in doubt, report—even if you are unsure whether something is an incident.
